From Contract Drafting to Dispute Prevention

Home / Insights / From Contract Drafting to Dispute Prevention

Youssef Badrawy Roshdy, International Guest Columnist

Menna Mohamed Ali, International Guest Columnist

Article

Published November 2026.

Share: WhatsApp Facebook X LinkedIn Email

1. Introduction

Commercial agreements allocate risks, define obligations, and structure business relationships. Ambiguous provisions, unclear performance standards, and inadequate remedies can create uncertainty and expose parties to costly disputes. Effective commercial lawyering therefore requires not only responding to breaches but also identifying potential conflicts during negotiation and drafting.

The UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts 2016 provide a framework for analysing contractual interpretation, performance, and non-performance. Their legal effect depends on the relevant contractual and legal context; they are not automatically binding law in every transaction.[1]

This article argues that dispute prevention should be an integral part of commercial contract drafting. It proposes a practical framework for identifying contractual risks, evaluating their consequences, and developing safeguards before an agreement is signed, while recognising that careful drafting cannot eliminate every dispute.

2. Identifying Legal Risks Before Signing

Effective contract review begins by identifying uncertainty in the parties’ obligations and the consequences of non-performance. Lawyers should examine the scope of work, delivery and payment deadlines, quality standards, notification procedures, liability provisions, and available remedies. These elements should be sufficiently clear to enable the parties to assess performance and respond to potential breaches.

Contractual interpretation may require more than examining the literal wording. Articles 4.1 and 4.3 of the UNIDROIT Principles identify the parties’ common intention and relevant circumstances, including preliminary negotiations, established practices, the contract’s purpose, and trade usages, as relevant considerations.[2]

The applicable legal framework must also be identified. Where the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG) applies, Article 35 addresses the conformity of goods with contractual requirements, while Articles 38 and 39 concern examination and notification of non-conformity.[3]

For example, unclear inspection procedures or inadequate arrangements for reporting defective goods may expose a buyer to avoidable legal risks.

Finally, lawyers should assess whether liability, termination, force majeure, and dispute-resolution provisions address foreseeable problems consistently. The objective is to identify material risks, evaluate their commercial consequences, and recommend proportionate safeguards before the parties become legally committed.

3. Drafting Protective and Enforceable Contract Clauses

Identifying risks is only the first step; effective drafting translates those risks into clear and legally appropriate contractual provisions. Agreements should define obligations, establish measurable performance standards, allocate foreseeable risks, and specify the consequences of non-performance. Their enforceability must be assessed under the applicable law.

Scope and performance clauses should specify the goods or services, technical standards, delivery dates, acceptance criteria, and respective responsibilities. Where the CISG applies, Article 35 provides a legal basis for assessing whether delivered goods conform to contractual requirements.[4]

Breach and remedies clauses should address defective performance, delayed delivery, and non-payment. Articles 45 and 61 of the CISG establish remedies available to buyers and sellers, respectively, subject to the Convention’s conditions.[5]

Liability and indemnity clauses should identify the risks covered, the losses recoverable, and any agreed limitations. Article 7.4.1 of the UNIDROIT Principles addresses the right to damages for non-performance, while Article 7.1.6 restricts reliance on certain exemption clauses where doing so would be grossly unfair in light of the contract’s purpose.[6]

Termination and force majeure clauses should specify applicable triggers, notification requirements, and the consequences of termination or impeded performance. Article 79 of the CISG provides an exemption from damages liability where its conditions are satisfied; it does not automatically terminate the contract or eliminate every remedy.[7]

Clear drafting should therefore address foreseeable disruptions without assuming that a general force majeure clause resolves every resulting legal issue.

4. Managing Disputes Through Contract Design

Effective contract design should establish practical mechanisms for preventing and resolving disputes. Clear procedures help parties understand their rights and obligations and reduce the possibility of costly legal conflicts.

One important mechanism is the notice of breach and cure period. The contract should specify how a party must notify the other of a breach and provide a reasonable period to correct it. Article 7.1.5 of the UNIDROIT Principles recognises the possibility of granting an additional period for performance, subject to specific conditions.[8]

Another safeguard is negotiation and amicable settlement. Parties may agree to resolve disagreements through direct negotiations or mediation before initiating legal proceedings. These methods can reduce costs and preserve commercial relationships.

The contract should also include a governing law clause identifying the legal system applicable to the agreement. The Hague Principles on Choice of Law in International Commercial Contracts 2015 recognise the parties’ freedom to choose the applicable law, subject to relevant limitations.[9]

Furthermore, jurisdiction and arbitration clauses should clearly determine how disputes will be resolved. Parties may choose national courts or arbitration. Article 6 of the CISG recognises the parties’ freedom to exclude the Convention or modify the effect of its provisions, subject to its requirements.[10]

The 1958 New York Convention also provides an important framework for international arbitration. Article II addresses the recognition of arbitration agreements, while Article III concerns the recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards.[11]

For example, an agreement between an Egyptian supplier and a foreign buyer may require negotiation within 30 days before referring unresolved disputes to arbitration. This creates a clear procedure and reduces uncertainty.

Ultimately, effective dispute management requires clear contractual procedures that protect the parties’ interests and support stable commercial relationships.

5. A Practical Risk-Review Framework for Lawyers

Effective contractual risk management requires a structured approach that helps lawyers identify potential problems and recommend preventive measures before an agreement is signed. This approach is consistent with international guidance on commercial contract preparation and risk allocation.[12]

  1. Understand: Understand the transaction, the client’s objectives, and the commercial expectations of the parties.
  2. Identify: Identify potential legal and commercial risks, including unclear obligations, delayed performance, payment failures, and liability issues.
  3. Assess: Evaluate each risk according to its likelihood and potential impact on the parties and the transaction.
  4. Mitigate: Recommend contractual amendments or preventive measures to reduce risks, such as clear deadlines, payment conditions, and appropriate liability provisions.
  5. Verify: Ensure that the final contractual clauses are clear, consistent, and enforceable under the applicable law.[13]

For example, if a supply agreement does not specify a delivery date, the lawyer should identify the risk of delay, assess its potential financial impact, and recommend including a clear delivery deadline and appropriate remedies for non-performance. This reflects the importance of precise contractual drafting in preventing commercial disputes.[14]

Applying these five steps enables lawyers to move beyond identifying contractual weaknesses toward developing practical solutions. It also helps protect clients’ interests, improve contractual certainty, and minimise foreseeable legal and commercial disputes.

6. Conclusion

Effective commercial contract drafting is essential for protecting the parties’ interests and reducing legal and commercial risks. This article has demonstrated that identifying potential risks, drafting clear and enforceable clauses, and establishing appropriate dispute-resolution mechanisms are fundamental elements of successful contractual relationships.

A proactive approach enables lawyers to anticipate potential conflicts rather than simply respond to them after they arise. By applying a structured risk-review framework, lawyers can improve contractual certainty, minimise misunderstandings, and develop practical solutions to foreseeable problems.

Ultimately, the value of a well-drafted commercial agreement lies not only in defining the parties’ rights and obligations but also in preventing disputes and promoting stability, trust, and long-term success in commercial relationships.
